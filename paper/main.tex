\title{On Ishiki's Conjecture~5.1:\\
       Non-Complete-Metrizability of $\Met(D)$ for $\lvert D\rvert=\aleph_1$}
\author{}
\date{\textit{Draft --- not for circulation}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  For a discrete topological space $D$, let $\Met(D)$ denote the set of
  metrics on $D$ that are compatible with the discrete topology, equipped
  with the topology induced by the supremum distance. Ishiki's
  Conjecture~5.1 asserts that $\Met(D)$ is not completely metrizable when
  $\lvert D\rvert = \aleph_1$. We prove this statement in ZFC, by an
  elementary argument that exhibits, inside $[0,1]$, a set of cardinality
  $\aleph_1$ that is not an $\Fsigma$ set, then transports the resulting
  non-$\Gdelta$ obstruction into $\Met(D)$ through an explicit closed
  embedding. The argument is accompanied by a machine-checked
  formalisation in Lean~4, described in the appendix.
\end{abstract}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Let $D$ be an infinite discrete topological space, and let $\Met(D)$
denote the set of metrics on $D$ that are \emph{compatible with the
discrete topology}: those $d$ for which the metric topology induced by
$d$ equals the discrete topology on $D$. This is a genuine restriction —
not every metric on the underlying set of $D$ induces the discrete
topology on it. We equip $\Met(D)$ with the topology induced by the
supremum distance
\[
  \rho(d, d') \;=\; \sup_{x,y\in D} \lvert d(x,y) - d'(x,y)\rvert ,
\]
which may take the value $+\infty$ for unbounded $d,d'$; this does not
affect complete metrizability, which is a property of a topology and not
of any particular compatible metric witnessing it (see
Remark~\ref{rem:extended-rho}).

Ishiki~\cite{Ishiki2024} recalls a theorem, attributed there to Koshino,
that for separable $X$, $\Met(X)$ is completely metrizable if and only if
$X$ is $\sigma$-compact, and proposes to remove the separability
hypothesis. Conjecture~5.1 of \cite{Ishiki2024} is the resulting question
for the least uncountable case:

\begin{quote}
  \emph{If $\lvert D\rvert = \aleph_1$, then $\Met(D)$ is not completely
  metrizable.}
\end{quote}

We prove this conjecture. The source paper also
raises several further questions about $\Met(D)$ for other cardinalities
and other topological properties; those are outside the scope of this
paper, which addresses only Conjecture~5.1 as stated above.

The proof strategy is the following. We first produce, using only ZFC (no
extra set-theoretic hypothesis), a set $A \subseteq [0,1]$ with
$\lvert A\rvert = \aleph_1$ that is not an $\Fsigma$ subset of $[0,1]$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:A-exists}). Its complement $A^\complement =
[0,1]\setminus A$ is then not a $\Gdelta$ subset of $[0,1]$, hence not
completely metrizable as a subspace of $[0,1]$. We then build an explicit
family $(d_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ of metrics on a discrete space $D$ of
cardinality $\aleph_1$ that lie in $\Met(D)$ exactly for $t \in
A^\complement$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:star-metric}), and show that $t \mapsto
d_t$ restricts to a bi-Lipschitz embedding of $A^\complement$ onto a
\emph{closed} subset of $\Met(D)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-image}). If
$\Met(D)$ were completely metrizable, this closed image would inherit
complete metrizability, and hence so would $A^\complement$ —
contradicting the first step. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:conj51}.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Notation}

Throughout, $\omega_1$ denotes the first uncountable ordinal, and
$\aleph_1 = \lvert\omega_1\rvert$ the first uncountable cardinal.
$\Cont := 2^{\aleph_0}$ denotes the cardinality of the continuum; by
Cantor's theorem $\aleph_0 < \Cont$, and since $\aleph_1$ is the least
uncountable cardinal, $\aleph_1 \le \Cont$ is a theorem of ZFC (whether or
not $\aleph_1 = \Cont$, i.e. the continuum hypothesis $\mathsf{CH}$,
holds). We write $\Fsigma$ (resp.\ $\Gdelta$) for the usual classes of
countable unions of closed sets (resp.\ countable intersections of open
sets), relative to whichever ambient space is under discussion. For $S
\subseteq [0,1]$ we write $S^\complement := [0,1] \setminus S$;
complements are always taken relative to $[0,1]$. A topological space is
\emph{completely metrizable} if it is homeomorphic to a complete metric
space; this is weaker than \emph{Polish}, which additionally requires
separability.

\subsection{Background results}
\label{subsec:background}

The proof uses two classical facts about complete metrizability:
Theorem~\ref{thm:background-gdelta} is applied to the ambient space
$[0,1]$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:background-closed} to $\Met(D)$.

\begin{theorem}[Alexandrov--Lavrentiev]
  \label{thm:background-gdelta}
  Let $X$ be a metrizable space and $S \subseteq X$. If $S$ is
  completely metrizable in the subspace topology, then $S$ is a $\Gdelta$
  subset of $X$.
\end{theorem}
This is the forward direction of the classical $\Gdelta$-characterisation
of complete metrizability, stated in this generality in
\cite[Theorem~3.11]{Kechris1995}; see also
\cite[Theorems~4.3.23--4.3.24]{Engelking1989} and
\cite[Theorem~24.12]{Willard2004}. We use only this direction, through
its contrapositive, applied in Section~\ref{sec:closed-image} to
$X = [0,1]$.

\begin{theorem}[Closed-subspace inheritance]
  \label{thm:background-closed}
  Let $X$ be a completely metrizable space and $Y \subseteq X$ a closed
  subset. Then $Y$, in the subspace topology, is completely metrizable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Fix a complete metric $d$ on $X$ inducing its topology. The restriction
  $d|_{Y\times Y}$ induces the subspace topology on $Y$, so it suffices to
  show that $d|_{Y\times Y}$ is complete. Let $(x_n)_n$ be a Cauchy
  sequence in $(Y, d|_Y)$. It is also a Cauchy sequence in $(X,d)$, so by
  completeness of $d$ it converges to some $x \in X$. In any metric space,
  a closed set is closed under limits of sequences of its own points, so
  $x \in Y$. Hence $(x_n)_n$ converges to $x$ within $(Y,d|_Y)$, which is
  therefore complete, and $Y$ is completely metrizable.
\end{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:background-closed} is the only fact about complete
metrizability that we apply to $\Met(D)$.

\begin{remark}[The supremum distance may be extended-valued]
  \label{rem:extended-rho}
  $\rho(d,d')$ can equal $+\infty$ when $d,d'$ are unbounded and their
  difference is unbounded. This does not affect any statement below:
  $\min(\rho,1)$ induces the same topology as $\rho$ and is an ordinary
  finite-valued metric, so $\Met(D)$ is metrizable in the usual sense, and
  complete metrizability is a topological property, independent of which
  compatible metric witnesses it. In any case, every metric constructed in
  Section~\ref{sec:main} takes values in $[0,2]$, so all instances of
  $\rho$ appearing there are finite.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$\Met(D)$ is not separable for $\lvert D\rvert = \aleph_1$]
  \label{rem:not-separable}
  Since $\aleph_1 = \aleph_1\cdot 2$, partition $D$ into $\aleph_1$
  disjoint pairs $\{u_\alpha,v_\alpha\}$ ($\alpha<\omega_1$). For
  $\varepsilon : \omega_1 \to \{1/3,\,2/3\}$, define $d_\varepsilon(u_\alpha,
  v_\alpha) := \varepsilon(\alpha)$ and $d_\varepsilon(x,y):=1$ for any two
  points $x,y$ lying in different pairs. Every triangle inequality
  involving a cross-pair edge holds, since a within-pair value lies in
  $\{1/3,2/3\}\subseteq(0,2)$ and $1 \le \tfrac13+1$; so $d_\varepsilon$ is
  a metric, and every point is at distance $\ge 1/3$ from every other
  point, so $d_\varepsilon \in \Met(D)$. If $\varepsilon \ne \varepsilon'$
  differ at some $\alpha$, then $\rho(d_\varepsilon,d_{\varepsilon'}) \ge
  \lvert\varepsilon(\alpha)-\varepsilon'(\alpha)\rvert = 1/3$, uniformly in
  $\alpha$. This gives $2^{\aleph_1}$ points of $\Met(D)$ pairwise at
  distance $\ge 1/3$; the open balls of radius $1/6$ around them are
  pairwise disjoint, so any dense subset of $\Met(D)$ must meet all
  $2^{\aleph_1}$ of them and hence be uncountable. So $\Met(D)$ is not
  separable. This fact plays no logical role in the proof below; it
  records that Conjecture~5.1 lies genuinely outside the separable
  setting of the theorem of Koshino recalled in the introduction.
\end{remark}

\section{Main Construction}
\label{sec:main}

\subsection{A non-$\Fsigma$ set of cardinality $\aleph_1$}
\label{subsec:A}

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:closed-card}
  If $F \subseteq [0,1]$ is closed and $\lvert F\rvert < \Cont$, then $F$
  is countable.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  We prove the contrapositive. Let $F$ be closed and uncountable. Being
  closed in $[0,1]$, $F$ is itself Polish, and by the Cantor--Bendixson
  theorem \cite[Theorem~6.4]{Kechris1995} it splits as a disjoint union
  $F = P \sqcup C$ of a perfect set $P$ and a countable set $C$. Since $F$
  is uncountable and $C$ is countable, $P \ne \emptyset$, and a nonempty
  perfect Polish space has cardinality $\Cont$
  \cite[Theorem~6.2, Corollary~6.3]{Kechris1995}; hence
  $\lvert F\rvert \ge \lvert P\rvert = \Cont$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:A-exists}
  There exists $A \subseteq [0,1]$ with $\lvert A\rvert = \aleph_1$
  such that $A$ is not an $\Fsigma$ set.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  We split on whether the continuum hypothesis $\mathsf{CH}$
  ($\aleph_1 = \Cont$) holds.

  \paragraph{Case 1: $\mathsf{CH}$ holds.} Let $A := [0,1]\setminus
  \mathbb{Q}$, the irrationals in $[0,1]$. Since $[0,1]$ has cardinality
  $\Cont$ and $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is countable, $\lvert A\rvert =
  \Cont = \aleph_1$, the last equality by $\mathsf{CH}$.

  $A$ is not $\Fsigma$ (this part does not use $\mathsf{CH}$): suppose
  $A = \bigcup_n F_n$ with each $F_n$ closed in $[0,1]$. Since
  $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is dense and $F_n \subseteq A$ is disjoint from
  it, each $F_n$ is a closed set with empty interior, hence nowhere
  dense; and $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is meagre as a countable union of
  singletons. So $[0,1] = A \cup (\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1])$ would be meagre
  in itself, contradicting the Baire category theorem
  \cite[Corollary~25.4]{Willard2004}. Hence $A$ is not $\Fsigma$.

  \paragraph{Case 2: $\mathsf{CH}$ fails, i.e.\ $\aleph_1 < \Cont$.}
  Since $\aleph_1 \le \Cont = \lvert[0,1]\rvert$, fix any $A \subseteq
  [0,1]$ with $\lvert A\rvert = \aleph_1$. Suppose $A = \bigcup_n F_n$
  with each $F_n$ closed in $[0,1]$. Then $\lvert F_n\rvert \le
  \lvert A\rvert = \aleph_1 < \Cont$ for every $n$, so each $F_n$ is
  countable by Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-card}. Hence $A$ is a countable
  union of countable sets, so countable — contradicting
  $\lvert A\rvert = \aleph_1$. So $A$ is not $\Fsigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  To avoid the case split on $\mathsf{CH}$, one may instead use a
  Bernstein set: a set $B \subseteq [0,1]$ such that $B$ and
  $B^\complement$ both meet every uncountable closed subset of $[0,1]$
  exists by transfinite recursion and has cardinality $\Cont$
  \cite[p.~24]{Oxtoby1980}. Take $A \subseteq B$ with $\lvert A\rvert =
  \aleph_1$. If $A = \bigcup_n F_n$ with each $F_n$ closed, then some
  $F_n$ is an uncountable closed set contained in $B$, contradicting
  that $B^\complement$ meets every uncountable closed set. This argument
  is uniform in $\mathsf{CH}$; we use the two-case argument above, which
  is fully elementary.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A parametrised family of metrics}
\label{subsec:star}

Fix, for the remainder of the paper, the set $A$ from
Lemma~\ref{lem:A-exists} together with an injective enumeration $A =
\{a_\alpha : \alpha < \omega_1\}$ (possible since $\lvert A\rvert =
\aleph_1$).

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:star-metric}
  There is a discrete space $D$ with $\lvert D\rvert = \aleph_1$ and a
  family $(d_t)_{t \in [0,1]}$ of metrics on $D$ such that $d_t \in
  \Met(D)$ holds if and only if $t \in A^\complement$.
\end{lemma}

Since a bijection of index sets induces an isometric isomorphism of
$\Met(D)$'s onto one another (compose a metric with the bijection on both
coordinates, which visibly preserves compatibility with the discrete
topology and the supremum distance), $\Met(D)$ up to isometric isomorphism
depends only on $\lvert D\rvert$; it therefore suffices to construct the
family for one convenient concrete $D$ of cardinality $\aleph_1$.

\paragraph{Construction.} Let
\[
  D := \{p,q\} \ \sqcup\ \{y_\alpha : \alpha<\omega_1\} \ \sqcup\
  \{x_{\alpha,n} : \alpha < \omega_1,\ n \in \mathbb{N}\},
\]
a disjoint union with $\lvert D\rvert = 2 + \aleph_1 + \aleph_1\cdot
\aleph_0 = \aleph_1$, carrying the discrete topology. For $\alpha <
\omega_1$ let $C_\alpha := \{y_\alpha\} \cup \{x_{\alpha,n} :
n\in\mathbb{N}\}$. Then
\[
  D = \{p,q\} \sqcup \bigsqcup_{\alpha<\omega_1} C_\alpha
\]
is a partition of $D$. For $t \in [0,1]$, $\alpha < \omega_1$,
$n \in \mathbb{N}$, set
\[
  s_n(\alpha,t) := \min\bigl(1,\ \lvert t-a_\alpha\rvert + 1/(n+1)\bigr)
  \in (0,1].
\]
Define $d_t : D \times D \to [0,2]$ by $d_t(u,u)=0$ and, on distinct
pairs, by

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Distinct pair $\{u,v\}$ & $d_t(u,v)$ \\
  \midrule
  $\{p,q\}$ & $1+t$ \\
  $\{y_\alpha, x_{\alpha,n}\}$ & $s_n(\alpha,t)$ \\
  $\{x_{\alpha,n},x_{\alpha,m}\}$, $n\ne m$ &
    $s_n(\alpha,t)+s_m(\alpha,t)$ \\
  Any other pair (two points in distinct parts of the partition) & $1$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The table assigns values to unordered pairs, so $d_t$ is symmetric; its
range lies in $[0,2]$ since $0 < s_n \le 1$ and $0\le t\le 1$.

\paragraph{(C1) $d_t$ is a metric, for every $t \in [0,1]$.} Definiteness
and symmetry are immediate; every table entry is strictly positive, so
$d_t(u,v)=0$ iff $u=v$. For the triangle inequality
$d_t(u,w)\le d_t(u,v)+d_t(v,w)$, the case where two of $u,v,w$ coincide is
trivial, so assume $u,v,w$ are pairwise distinct, and abbreviate $s_n :=
s_n(\alpha,t)$ once $\alpha$ is fixed. We use two facts read off the
table: every distance is at most $2$, and two points in distinct parts of
the partition are at distance exactly $1$. Split on how $\{u,v,w\}$ meets
the partition.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{All three in one part.} Since $\{p,q\}$ has only two
  elements, the part is some $C_\alpha$. If the three points are
  $y_\alpha$, $x_{\alpha,n}$, $x_{\alpha,m}$ ($n \ne m$), the required
  inequalities are $s_n+s_m \le s_n + s_m$ (through $y_\alpha$) and
  $s_n \le (s_n+s_m) + s_m$ (through $x_{\alpha,m}$); the remaining
  orderings are symmetric copies of these two. If the three points are
  $x_{\alpha,n},x_{\alpha,m},x_{\alpha,k}$ (distinct indices), then
  $s_n+s_m \le (s_n+s_k) + (s_k+s_m)$.
\item \emph{Exactly two in one part $E$, one point $z$ outside $E$.} Then
  $d_t(u,z)=d_t(v,z)=1$ for the two points $u,v \in E$, so
  $d_t(u,v)\le 2 = d_t(u,z)+d_t(z,v)$ and $d_t(u,z)=1\le d_t(u,v)+1 =
  d_t(u,v)+d_t(v,z)$.
\item \emph{Three distinct parts.} All three pairwise distances equal
  $1$, and $1\le 1+1$.
\end{itemize}
These cases exhaust the possible distributions of three points over the
partition, so $d_t$ satisfies the triangle inequality and is a metric.

\paragraph{(C2) $t \in A^\complement \implies d_t \in \Met(D)$.} A metric
induces the discrete topology on $D$ exactly when every point is
isolated, i.e. $\delta(u) := \inf_{v\ne u} d_t(u,v) > 0$ for every $u \in
D$. Fix $t \in A^\complement$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\delta(p)=\delta(q)=1$: the distance from $p$ (resp.\ $q$) to any
  point other than $q$ (resp.\ $p$) is $1$, and $d_t(p,q)=1+t\ge 1$.
\item For $x_{\alpha,n}$: its distances are $s_n(\alpha,t)$
  (to $y_\alpha$), $s_n(\alpha,t)+s_m(\alpha,t) \ge s_n(\alpha,t)$ (to
  $x_{\alpha,m}$, $m \ne n$), and $1$ (outside $C_\alpha$); since $s_n
  \le 1$, $\delta(x_{\alpha,n}) = s_n(\alpha,t) \ge 1/(n+1)>0$.
\item For $y_\alpha$: its distances are $s_n(\alpha,t)$
  ($n\in\mathbb{N}$), and $1$ outside $C_\alpha$.
  Since $r\mapsto\min(1,r)$ is continuous and nondecreasing and
  $\lvert t-a_\alpha\rvert+1/(n+1) \downarrow \lvert t-a_\alpha\rvert$ as
  $n\to\infty$, $\inf_n s_n(\alpha,t) = \min(1,\lvert t-a_\alpha\rvert)$,
  so $\delta(y_\alpha)=\min(1,\lvert t-a_\alpha\rvert)$. As $t \in
  A^\complement$ and $a_\alpha \in A$, $t \ne a_\alpha$, so
  $\delta(y_\alpha)>0$.
\end{itemize}
Every point is isolated, so $d_t$ induces the discrete topology; with
(C1), $d_t \in \Met(D)$.

\paragraph{(C3) $t \in A \implies d_t \notin \Met(D)$.} Let $t=a_\beta \in
A$. By (C1), $d_t$ is still a metric. But $s_n(\beta,a_\beta) =
\min(1,1/(n+1))=1/(n+1) \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$, so
$d_t(y_\beta,x_{\beta,n}) = 1/(n+1)$: every ball around $y_\beta$ of
radius $\varepsilon>0$ contains some $x_{\beta,n}\ne y_\beta$ once
$1/(n+1)<\varepsilon$. So $\{y_\beta\}$ is not open, and $d_t$ does not
induce the discrete topology, i.e.\ $d_t \notin \Met(D)$.

Combining (C2) and (C3): for $t \in [0,1]$, $d_t \in \Met(D)$ if and only
if $t \in A^\complement$. This proves Lemma~\ref{lem:star-metric}.

\begin{remark}
  \label{rem:star-name}
  By the equality case in (C1), the distance between two points
  $x_{\alpha,n}$ and $x_{\alpha,m}$ of a common $C_\alpha$ is realised by
  the path through $y_\alpha$, so each $(C_\alpha, d_t)$ is isometric to
  a metric star. This geometric feature is not used in the sequel; it
  gives the construction its name (\texttt{star}) in the Lean
  formalisation described in the appendix.
\end{remark}

\section{Proof of the Main Theorem}
\label{sec:closed-image}

We continue to use the $A$, $D$, and $(d_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ fixed in
Section~\ref{subsec:star}. Give $A^\complement$ the subspace topology
from $[0,1]$ (equivalently, the metric $\lvert t-s\rvert$).

\begin{lemma}
  \label{lem:closed-image}
  The map $\varphi : A^\complement \to \Met(D)$, $\varphi(t) := d_t$, is a
  bi-Lipschitz embedding, and its image $\varphi(A^\complement)$ is
  closed in $\Met(D)$.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{(C4) Bi-Lipschitz estimate.} For all $s,t\in[0,1]$,
\[
  \lvert t-s\rvert \ \le\ \rho(d_t,d_s) \ \le\ 2\lvert t-s\rvert .
\]
\emph{Lower bound.} Comparing values on the pair $\{p,q\}$,
$\lvert d_t(p,q)-d_s(p,q)\rvert = \lvert(1+t)-(1+s)\rvert = \lvert
t-s\rvert$, and $\rho(d_t,d_s)$ is at least this one value.

\emph{Upper bound.} It suffices to bound $\lvert d_t(u,v)-d_s(u,v)\rvert
\le 2\lvert t-s\rvert$ for every pair $\{u,v\}$ and take the supremum. On
$\{p,q\}$ the difference is $\lvert t-s\rvert$. The map $r
\mapsto \min(1,r)$ is $1$-Lipschitz, and $r \mapsto \lvert r-a_\alpha\rvert
+ 1/(n+1)$ is $1$-Lipschitz by the reverse triangle inequality, so their
composite $r \mapsto s_n(\alpha,r)$ is $1$-Lipschitz: $\lvert
s_n(\alpha,t)-s_n(\alpha,s)\rvert \le \lvert t-s\rvert$. Hence for a
pair $\{y_\alpha, x_{\alpha,n}\}$ the difference is at most $\lvert
t-s\rvert \le 2\lvert t-s\rvert$, and for a pair
$\{x_{\alpha,n},x_{\alpha,m}\}$ it is at most $2\lvert t-s\rvert$ by the
triangle inequality on two such terms. For pairs meeting two distinct
parts of the partition the value is constantly $1$, so the difference is
$0$. This proves the upper bound, hence (C4).

Since $\varphi$ is injective by the lower bound and both Lipschitz
estimates hold, $\varphi$ is a bi-Lipschitz bijection from
$A^\complement$ onto $\varphi(A^\complement)$ (with the subspace topology
from $\Met(D)$), in particular a homeomorphism onto its image.

\paragraph{(C5) The image is closed.} The topology of $\Met(D)$ is given
by the finite metric $\min(\rho,1)$, so closure points are sequential
limits. Let $e \in \Met(D)$ be a limit point of
$\varphi(A^\complement)$, and choose $t_k \in A^\complement$ with
$\rho(d_{t_k},e)\to 0$; we show $e \in \varphi(A^\complement)$.

\emph{Recovering the parameter.} $\lvert d_{t_k}(p,q)-e(p,q)\rvert \le
\rho(d_{t_k},e)\to 0$ gives $1+t_k \to e(p,q)$, i.e.\ $t_k \to t$ for $t :=
e(p,q)-1$. Each $t_k \in [0,1]$ and $[0,1]$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}$, so
$t \in [0,1]$, and by (C1) $d_t$ is a well-defined metric on $D$ (not yet
known to lie in $\Met(D)$).

\emph{Identifying the limit.} Fix $u,v \in D$. By (C4),
$\rho(d_{t_k},d_t) \le 2\lvert t_k-t\rvert \to 0$, so
\[
  \lvert e(u,v)-d_t(u,v)\rvert \le \rho(e,d_{t_k}) +
  \rho(d_{t_k},d_t) \longrightarrow 0 \quad (k \to \infty),
\]
while the left side does not depend on $k$; hence $e(u,v)=d_t(u,v)$ for
all $u,v$, i.e.\ $e = d_t$.

\emph{Excluding the boundary case.} By hypothesis $e \in \Met(D)$, so
$d_t = e \in \Met(D)$. If $t \in A$, (C3) gives $d_t \notin \Met(D)$, a
contradiction; so $t \notin A$, i.e.\ $t \in A^\complement$, and $e = d_t
= \varphi(t) \in \varphi(A^\complement)$.

Thus $\varphi(A^\complement)$ contains all its closure points and is
closed in $\Met(D)$. This proves Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-image}. Excluding
the case $t \in A$ — where $d_t$ remains a metric but no longer induces
the discrete topology — is the substance of the closedness, and is
exactly what (C3) provides.

\begin{theorem}[Ishiki's Conjecture~5.1]
  \label{thm:conj51}
  If $\lvert D\rvert = \aleph_1$, then $\Met(D)$, with the supremum-metric
  topology, is not completely metrizable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By Section~\ref{subsec:star}, $\Met(D)$ up to isometric isomorphism
  depends only on $\lvert D\rvert$, so it suffices to treat the concrete
  $D$ of Section~\ref{subsec:star}.
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item By Lemma~\ref{lem:A-exists}, fix $A\subseteq[0,1]$ with $\lvert
    A\rvert=\aleph_1$ that is not $\Fsigma$.
  \item Since $A$ is not $\Fsigma$ in $[0,1]$, its complement
    $A^\complement$ is not $\Gdelta$ in $[0,1]$, by the termwise duality
    of the two classes under complementation.
  \item $[0,1]$ is metrizable, so the contrapositive of
    Theorem~\ref{thm:background-gdelta} applies: $A^\complement$ is not
    completely metrizable as a subspace of $[0,1]$.
  \item By Lemma~\ref{lem:star-metric} and Lemma~\ref{lem:closed-image},
    $\varphi : A^\complement \to \Met(D)$ is a homeomorphism onto its
    image $\varphi(A^\complement)$, which is closed in $\Met(D)$.
  \item Suppose, for contradiction, that $\Met(D)$ is completely
    metrizable. By Theorem~\ref{thm:background-closed}, the closed subset
    $\varphi(A^\complement)$ is then also completely metrizable. Complete
    metrizability is a topological invariant, so the homeomorphism of
    step 4 transports it back to $A^\complement$: $A^\complement$ would
    be completely metrizable, contradicting step 3.
  \item Hence $\Met(D)$ is not completely metrizable. \qedhere
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concluding}

Theorem~\ref{thm:conj51} is a theorem of ZFC: no set-theoretic hypothesis
beyond ZFC is assumed anywhere in the proof. The case split on
$\mathsf{CH}$ inside the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:A-exists}
(Section~\ref{subsec:A}) is a proof technique — both branches are proved
outright, and exactly one of them applies in any given model of ZFC — not
an additional hypothesis on $D$ or on the ambient set theory.

The argument is also machine-checked: it
is formalised in the Lean~4 proof assistant, checked against Mathlib, and
is sorry-free (no unproved gaps and no project-specific axioms). The
formalisation is described in the Appendix; it serves as a
reproducibility and proof-assurance artifact for the mathematical content
above, not as an independent presentation of the result.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The theorem recalled in the introduction, attributed to Koshino, situates
Theorem~\ref{thm:conj51} as the removal of a separability hypothesis from
a known separable characterisation; a different, independently published
result of Koshino concerns recognising the topology of $\Met(X)$ from data
about the topology of uniform convergence \cite{Koshino2022}. Ishiki
\cite{Ishiki2024Baire} proves a genericity (comeagreness) result about
$\Met(X)$ inside a larger ambient space of pseudometric-type objects; this
result is used in \cite{Ishiki2024}'s proof of its own Theorem~1.1, but
plays no role in the proof given here.
